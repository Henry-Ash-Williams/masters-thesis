Malicious software, otherwise known as malware, are widely considered to be one of the most pervasive dangers faced by users in the current digital threat landscape~\cite{europeanunionagencyforcybersecurity.ENISAThreatLandscape2024}. In recent years, the prevelance of this form of software has been on the increase, with nearly 100 million samples seen since the start of 2024 alone~(Figure~\ref{fig:malware-in-the-wild}). In order to mitigate this risk, detection of these programs is of the utmost importance to security researchers, and has been an active area of research within the field~\cite{manirihoSystematicLiteratureReview2024}. 

In this literature review, we explore some of the defining characteristics of malware, how threat actors evade detection, and a number of proposed approaches leveraging machine learning to detect these programs. 

\subsection{Malware}

Malware are defined as programs which perform unwanted or harmful actions on a host system without the consent or knowledge of its user. 

The actions of these programs on an infected system vary, but they all have an adverse affect on the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of a system. These properties comprise the core tenet of information security, known as the CIA triad, which guide policy for ensuring the security of a computer systems. Violation of this principle can provide hostile actors access to sensitive information, the ability to modify data, or the disruption of regular operation, all without necessary authorisation. 

Security researchers often classify these programs based on their propagation method and actions on its host. It is worth noting however, that the categories within this taxonomy are not mutually exclusive, and modern malware often exist in multiple categories at once. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Worm} | Malicious software which propagate throughout a network of interconnected systems. 
    \item \textbf{Virus} | Programs that modify other programs in order to replicate throughout an infected system.
    \item \textbf{Trojans} | Malware that disguises itself as legitimate software in order to infect its host.
    \item \textbf{Backdoor} | Software that provide covert remote access to infected systems. 
    \item \textbf{Ransomware} | Malware that deny access to systems or information until a fee is paid.
    \item \textbf{Information Stealer} | Malware which automatically extract information, such as credentials, and the activities of users. 
    \item \textbf{Botnet} | Often part of a larger network of infected systems, controlled by a threat actor to perform large scale attacks against digital infrastructure.
    \item \textbf{Rootkit} | Malware providing privileged access to an infected system.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Obfuscation}

% Why do malware authors obfuscate their programs? 
Commercial anti-malware services utilise a set of heuristics that are often indicative of malware. An example of such a heuristic could be the presence of a certain static string, or a series of bytes at a given offset. A large collection of these rules are maintained by security researchers, which provides an efficient and accurate means to detect malware. 

% What does obfuscation mean? 
In order to evade detection by these heuristics, malware authors utilise a technique known as obfuscation that modify the structure of the program, while preserving its intended behaviour~\cite{youMalwareObfuscationTechniques2010}. By modifying the structure, it will not match these pre-defined rules, thereby avoiding detection. In addition to modifying the structure of a program, it is also possible to modify its characteristics to evade detection by other more sophisticated dynamic approaches~\cite{liAPIASONovelAPI2023}.

\subsection{Detection}

A number of approaches to malware detection have been proposed in the literature. These largely fall into three categories, based on how features are extracted for later use for classification, these are as follows: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Static} | Features extracted from the structure of a program, including metadata, instructions, and data found within the executable. 
    \item \textbf{Dynamic} | Features extracted during the execution of the program, including external function calls, communication, and modifications made to the host. 
    \item \textbf{Hybrid} | A combination of both \emph{static} and \emph{dynamic} features 
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Static}

Static detection methods leverage features derived from the structure of the program rather than its behaviour during execution. Much of the literature on static malware detection leverage features such as sequences of instructions executed by the CPU~\cite{attaluriProfileHiddenMarkov2009,runwalOpcodeGraphSimilarity2012}, printable strings~\cite{shijoIntegratedStaticDynamic2015}, the external functions and libraries used by a program~\cite{schultzDataMiningMethods2001a}, and the executables header~\cite{raviMultiViewAttentionbasedDeep2022}. This form of malware detection is known to be more efficient than dynamic methods, however it can be less robust to obfuscation.

In~\cite{schultzDataMiningMethods2001a}, the authors propose making use of static features including printable strings, external functions, and byte sequences to train conventional machine learning algoriths to detect malware. In their evaluation, they found that the naive bayes algorithm, trained on the static strings of an executable gives the best performance, with 97\% classification accuracy. In addition to this, they also tested an inductive rule learner known as RIPPER, trained on information regarding the use of external functions in a program. This model was able to reliably detect malware approximately 90\% of the time, and we believe this to be a result of a high degree of overlap between the external functions used by malicious and benign programs. This indicates that these features may not be suitable for malware detection. Furthermore, the dataset used in their research was limited and unbalanced, with 3265 malicious, and only 1001 benign samples. This would likely have an adverse effect on the generalisability of their proposed approach. 

A great deal of previous research into static malware detection utilise Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), trained on the graphical representation of a program~\cite{suLightweightClassificationIoT2018,niMalwareIdentificationUsing2018,jainConvolutionalNeuralNetworks2020,manirihoSystematicLiteratureReview2024}. These have shown to be a promising approach to this problem, as CNNs are effective at learning a wide range of classification problems~\cite{habibiPerformanceEvaluationCNN2023,krizhevskyImageNetClassificationDeep2017}. However, these models require an image of fixed resolution. In the case that an executable is significantly larger than other samples in the dataset, it would be necessary to resize the image, which is a lossy operation. This may lead to important features being lost, and making it more challenging to accurately classify the program. 

Much of the literature that consider opcode sequences for malware detection focus on its use in traditional machine learning algorithms~\cite{attaluriProfileHiddenMarkov2009,runwalOpcodeGraphSimilarity2012}. These approaches both make use of Hidden Markov Models, and achieve promising results, indicating instruction sequences are a viable feature for detection. Extending upon this idea,~\citeauthor{demirciStaticMalwareDetection2022}~\cite{demirciStaticMalwareDetection2022} outline an approach in which they propose the use of stacked bidirectional Long-Short Term Memory (BiLSTM) and pre-trained language models including BERT~\cite{devlinBERTPretrainingDeep2019a}, and GPT-2~\cite{radfordLanguageModelsArea}. Their BiLSTM model was able to achieve 98\% classification accuracy whereas the pre-trained transformer models achieved only 77\%. As transformer models have been shown to be a capable method in sequence classification problems, this result is surprising. We believe this to be a result of the corpora used to pre-train the transformer models used in their investigation, which are comprised of mostly english texts. This would mean that these models would have a poor understanding of assembly instructions, making classification difficult. 

Previous research has also established the use of pre-trained language models in malware detection on the metadata of an executable. In~\cite{rahaliMalBERTMalwareDetection2021},~\citeauthor{rahaliMalBERTMalwareDetection2021} fine-tune BERT on text gathered from the manifest file found in android applications. This file contains information pertaining to the activities, background tasks, event listeners, and permissions granted to an application. Their model was able to achieve 97\% binary classification accuracy, in comparison to~\cite{demirciStaticMalwareDetection2022} which was only able to achieve 77\% with the same model, trained on instruction sequences. We believe this discrepancy to be a result of the language found in these manifest files being more closely related to the pre-training corpora of the BERT model. This would enable their model to better understand the information found in the manifest, and its use in distinguishing between benign and malicious executables. Their approach however, is limited to environments where a file containing metadata are required, such as mobile operating systems, making it unsuitable for desktop malware detection. 

\subsubsection{Dynamic}

In comparison to static methods, dynamic methods consider the behavioral characteristics of a program during its execution. This requires the use of an isolated environment, such as a virtual machine to safely execute a program to observe its behavior~\cite{botacinChallengesPitfallsMalware2021}. These approaches are known to be more resiliant to obfuscation, at the high cost of complexity and computational resources. 

Our research indicates that much of the work in this field leverage API call sequences to distinguish between malicious and benign programs. This enables these approaches to develop an understanding of actions taken by a program on a host system, and what actions may be indicative of a malicious program. This has been shown to be an effective approach to dynamic malware detection. For example, in~\cite{kiNovelApproachDetect2015}, the authors propose a signature based approach that was shown to be highly accurate in their experiments with an accuracy of approximately 99\%. The authors curate a database of subsequences often associated with malicious programs through the use of multi-sequence alignment. However, we do not believe their approach would be resiliant to obfuscation, as a malware developer could simply insert junk api calls in between other important calls to avoid detection by the proposed approach. 

In their \citeyear{liDMalNetDynamicMalware2022} paper, \citeauthor{liDMalNetDynamicMalware2022}~\cite{liDMalNetDynamicMalware2022}, the authors propose a detection method that also makes use of API call sequence information. However, they extend upon previous research by providing this information in the form of a graph, where shared arguments between two consecuitive api calls are given as edge data. A graph neural network is then trained to classify this information, and was able to achieve 98\% accuracy over a large dataset of 40,000 samples. In comparison to~\cite{kiNovelApproachDetect2015}, junk API calls would be disconnected in the graph, and the model could easily learn to ignore any such elements.

However, some forms of malware obfuscate their API calls, effectively defeating these approaches~\cite{liAPIASONovelAPI2023}. As a result of this, other features that are more resiliant to obfuscation have been explored. For example, in~\cite{doxuanNewApproachAPT2022}, the authors outline an approach that again makes use of graph neural networks. However, instead of considering just API calls, they develop a behavioral profile in order to detect highly sophisticated malware, such as those developed by Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs). APTs are often sponsored by a nation state, and thus have access to advanced tools, such as zero-day exploits, and advanced obfuscation techniques, making detection challenging. This behavioral profile is based on system events triggered by a program, and includes information relating to the creation of processes, changes made to the file system and registry, and any network communications. This method is able to reliably detect these sophisticated forms of malware, and is resiliant to advanced forms of obfuscation. However, this comes at a high cost in terms of computational resources. 

\subsection{Conclusions and Future Research Directions}

From our research, we have found that there are a number of approaches to malware detection that utilise a variety of features. 

Static approaches, whereby only the structure of an executable is considered rely heavily on image-based representations of programs, which a CNN can then be trained on. The success of these approaches vary, but we found that as a result of variability in the size of an executable, important features may be lost when resizing the image to a fixed resolution. 

We have also found that sequential models, that learn to detect malware based on the instructions performed throughout the course of a program execution are a promising area of investigation. The BiLSTM model proposed in~\cite{demirciStaticMalwareDetection2022} achieves high detection accuracy, and also suggest that pre-trained transformer models, such as BERT are less suitable to this task. However, these models have been shown to be well suited to sequence classification, and we believe that they could be a viable approach to malware detection if they were to be pre-trained on assembly instructions rather than natural languages such as english. Other approaches that levearge transformer architectures seem to verify this claim, as seen in~\cite{rahaliMalBERTMalwareDetection2021}, where a transformer architecture was fine-tuned to classify malware using the manifest file found in android executables. The contents of this file are more similar to the language used in pre-training, and their model would thus have a better understanding of the file, enhancing its classification accuracy. 

Dynamic detection methods on the other hand, despite being more resource intensive, seem to be more capable to detecting heavily obfuscated executables, through the observation of an executables behavior rather than its structure. Much of the work done in this area of the field extract sequences of API calls made during a programs execution, upon which the executable can be classified. This is a powerful approach to malware detection, however significant infrastructure is necessary to safely execute a program in an isolated environment. Furthermore, it is possible to obfuscate API calls, as shown in~\cite{liAPIASONovelAPI2023} that degrade the effectiveness of this approach. While it is possible to develop behavioral profiles to aid these approaches resiliance to obfuscation, this comes at a high cost in computation. 

We believe that by pre-training our own transformer based model, such as BERT, on assembly instructions, and then fine tuning this model to classify programs, we can achieve a good balance between computational efficiency, and detection accuracy. We will also investigate this models resiliance to obfuscation, by obfuscating our benign programs to varying degrees.
